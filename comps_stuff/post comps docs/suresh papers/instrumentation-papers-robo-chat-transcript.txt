[11:46:06 PM] Robo: Great question, and here's the crucial distinction I glossed over. All three give you forces, yes, but there are two really different ways of getting there.

Bota and FlexiForce are DIRECT transducers. Bota uses strain gauges, FlexiForce uses a piezoresistive element — both convert mechanical stress directly into an electrical signal that gets calibrated to force. You push, voltage changes, you read force. Physical transduction, traceable calibration.

GelSight is INDIRECT. The camera doesn't see force at all — it sees deformation of the gel. Force is then computed backwards by saying "the gel has this known elasticity, it's deformed this much, so the force applied must have been X." It's a model-based inference, not a direct reading. Which means: if your gel properties drift, or temperature changes, or the gel wears down, your force estimate drifts too.

So differences beyond just "direction or not":

Bota: 6-DOF directly measured force, high bandwidth one kilohertz, very accurate, single point. Expensive — five to ten thousand.

GelSight: dense spatial field of normal AND shear over a whole contact patch, camera-bandwidth limited — usually 30 to 60 hertz, accuracy depends on gel model. Middle cost. The thing it really shines at is getting a spatial pressure map PLUS the actual deformation field simultaneously — one sensor, two modalities.

FlexiForce: cheap, scalar normal force only, no direction, known to drift and have hysteresis. Tens of dollars. Good for cheap monitoring, not for precision work.

For your PAT-Scan pipeline, the real tradeoff is: do you need directional precision at a single point (Bota) or dense spatial distribution (GelSight) over a contact patch?
