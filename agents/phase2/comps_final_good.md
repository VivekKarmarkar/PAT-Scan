## B. Significance

### The Clinical Challenge of Tissue Stiffness Measurement

Tissue stiffness—invisible to the naked eye and intangible except through touch—determines how biological materials respond to applied forces, making it a powerful diagnostic marker across medicine. Palpation, the clinical practice of detecting abnormalities through touch, has guided medical diagnosis for centuries and remains a cornerstone of physical examination. In oncology, the ability to detect stiff nodules embedded in soft tissue proves particularly valuable: breast cancer lumps exhibit stiffness values 5 to 10 times greater than surrounding healthy tissue, creating a mechanical contrast that skilled clinicians can sometimes identify through manual examination alone.

Yet manual palpation suffers from fundamental limitations that constrain its diagnostic utility. The technique is inherently subjective, varying with examiner experience and technique. It cannot quantify stiffness values, providing only qualitative assessments of "hard" versus "soft." Access is limited to superficial lesions within reach of fingertip palpation, typically no deeper than 2-3 centimeters below the skin surface. Perhaps most critically, small or deeply embedded abnormalities may go undetected entirely, particularly in patients where overlying tissue density impedes mechanical interrogation.

Modern elastography techniques emerged to address these limitations by providing quantitative stiffness maps of internal tissue structures. Magnetic Resonance Elastography (MRE) offers high accuracy and full three-dimensional visualization, but requires specialized equipment costing approximately $2 million, effectively limiting its availability to major medical centers in well-resourced regions. Ultrasound elastography provides a more affordable alternative at around $100,000 per system, yet remains operator-dependent and challenging to standardize across institutions and practitioners. This accessibility gap proves particularly acute in resource-constrained settings worldwide, where the absence of quantitative tissue characterization significantly impacts diagnostic outcomes, treatment planning decisions, and ultimately patient survival rates.

### The Inverse Problem in Continuum Mechanics

At its mathematical core, tissue stiffness reconstruction constitutes an inverse problem in continuum mechanics. The forward problem—computing displacement fields from known material properties and applied forces—represents well-established physics. Finite element methods solve the linear elasticity equations F = KU with high accuracy and computational efficiency, and experimental validation has confirmed their predictions across countless applications in structural mechanics, biomedical engineering, and materials science. The mathematical framework is sound, the numerical implementations are mature, and confidence in forward model predictions runs appropriately high.

The inverse problem presents an entirely different challenge. Inferring material properties from measured displacements reverses the causality arrow, transforming a well-posed mathematical problem into one that is fundamentally ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard. Solutions may not be unique: multiple different material property distributions can produce nearly identical boundary displacement patterns. Extreme sensitivity to measurement noise amplifies this difficulty—small errors in displacement measurements can lead to large errors in reconstructed stiffness values, particularly when regularization is insufficient or poorly chosen.

Traditional approaches to elasticity inverse problems fall broadly into two categories, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Iterative optimization methods, exemplified by the mechanics-based tomography work of Goenezen and colleagues, minimize the residual between measured and predicted displacements by adjusting element-wise material properties through adjoint-based gradient descent. While mathematically rigorous and capable of handling complex geometries, these approaches prove computationally expensive, requiring repeated finite element solutions at each optimization iteration. Local minima in the optimization landscape pose additional challenges, with solution quality often depending sensitively on initial conditions. Such methods typically require full-field displacement measurements from the interior of the sample, necessitating specialized imaging systems capable of capturing deformation throughout the entire volume.

Konofagou and Harrigan introduced a key insight in their 2003 palpation tomography work that proved foundational for subsequent research. They demonstrated that applying multiple distinct loading configurations rather than a single large-area compression "increased the ratio of measurements to fitted parameters, which made the method less sensitive to random errors." Their experiments showed that using nine loading cases achieved noise reduction by a factor of two compared to single-load elastography. However, their approach still required full-field internal displacement measurements via ultrasound imaging and relied on element-wise iterative optimization, constraining both the hardware requirements and computational efficiency.

More recently, Katie Bouman and colleagues demonstrated through Visual Vibration Tomography that material properties can be inferred from observed motion patterns captured in ordinary video. This elegant approach uses vibration modes as the interrogation mechanism, extracting sub-pixel motion from monocular high-speed video to constrain physics-based inverse models. The dynamic elastography framework achieves impressive results on laboratory specimens and introduces powerful computational techniques for physics-informed reconstruction. Our work differs by employing quasi-static loading rather than dynamic vibrations, which simplifies both the actuation hardware (no vibration exciters required) and the forward model physics (no need for modal analysis or dynamic terms in the governing equations).

The knowledge gap that PAT-Scan addresses lies at the intersection of three critical requirements: boundary-only displacement measurements compatible with surface imaging systems like Digital Image Correlation, physics-informed learning that preserves mechanical plausibility through explicit enforcement of equilibrium, and the ability to handle arbitrary irregular geometries without geometric assumptions or parametric shape models. No existing elastography method simultaneously satisfies all three criteria. MRE provides excellent accuracy but requires expensive internal imaging. Palpation tomography pioneered multiple loading protocols but demands full-field displacement data. Visual Vibration Tomography works from boundary observations but operates in the dynamic regime with attendant complexity. PAT-Scan occupies the previously unexplored space where these requirements converge.

### Positioning PAT-Scan in the Elastography Landscape

Understanding where PAT-Scan fits in the landscape of elastography techniques requires examining the fundamental trade-offs between equipment cost, spatial resolution, depth penetration, and data requirements. MRE achieves approximately 2 millimeter spatial resolution with full-body depth penetration, but demands $2 million in specialized MRI hardware and pulse sequences. Ultrasound elastography offers finer resolution near 1 millimeter and reasonable depth penetration up to 10 centimeters, yet requires $100,000 systems operated by trained sonographers. PAT-Scan trades some of this resolution and depth capability for dramatic cost reduction, targeting mesh-dependent resolution in the 2-5 millimeter range with surface-biased sensitivity, all achieved with approximately $10,000 in hardware consisting of Digital Image Correlation cameras and calibrated force sensors.

Rural clinics in developed countries and healthcare facilities throughout the developing world might reasonably afford a PAT-Scan system where MRE costs prove prohibitive.

The approach does carry inherent limitations that must be acknowledged candidly. Measurements are fundamentally surface-biased, with displacement information concentrated at the boundary where forces are applied and imaging occurs. This limits depth penetration compared to MRE's ability to interrogate tissues throughout the entire body volume. PAT-Scan requires accurate boundary displacement measurement, demanding careful Digital Image Correlation system calibration to achieve the sub-pixel resolution needed for successful reconstruction. Intraoperative guidance presents a second promising application. Surgical palpation, where the surgeon's finger provides the interrogating force, could be augmented with surface camera imaging to enable real-time inclusion localization during procedures. MRE and ultrasound are inherently pre-operative imaging modalities; PAT-Scan could complement these by providing updated stiffness information as the surgical field evolves, tissues are retracted, and anatomical relationships shift during the operation itself. The fundamental trade-off must be stated clearly: PAT-Scan sacrifices three-dimensional full-field resolution for equipment accessibility and cost reduction. ## C. Innovation

### Innovation Framework: Three-Part Methodological Contribution

PAT-Scan introduces a three-part methodological innovation that distinguishes it from prior work in inverse elastography. First, we reformulate the traditional continuous material field estimation problem as a geometric segmentation task for systems with discrete material regions, dramatically reducing dimensionality while maintaining clinical relevance for tumor detection and defect localization. Second, we employ a hybrid physics-machine learning architecture that decouples finite element forward solving from neural network inverse learning, achieving computational efficiency while preserving physical rigor. Third, we implement a systematic interrogation strategy inspired by computed tomography, applying forces sequentially at varied angular positions while incorporating universal geometry handling that adapts automatically to circular, elliptical, or irregular inclusion shapes without algorithm modification.

These three innovations combine to achieve computational cost reductions of 10 to 100 times compared to iterative optimization methods while maintaining physical plausibility through exact finite element constraint satisfaction. The following sections detail each innovation and its scientific contribution.

### Innovation 1: Geometric Inverse Problem Reformulation

Traditional elasticity inverse methods, including the mechanics-based tomography approach of Goenezen and colleagues and the adjoint-based techniques developed by Oberai and coworkers, formulate the problem as continuous material field estimation. The goal is to recover spatially-varying Young's modulus E(x,y) at every element throughout the domain—a high-dimensional optimization problem requiring careful regularization to prevent overfitting to noise. With typical finite element meshes containing 1,000 to 10,000 elements, the unknown parameter space has corresponding dimensionality, creating substantial computational challenges and requiring extensive measurement data to constrain the solution adequately.

PAT-Scan reformulates the problem for systems with piecewise constant material properties: a stiff inclusion embedded in a soft background, each with distinct but spatially uniform Young's moduli. Rather than solving for continuous E(x,y) throughout the domain, the geometric inverse problem seeks to identify the inclusion boundary—the curve separating the two material regions. This reformulation reduces the unknown from a continuous field requiring thousands of parameters to a geometric boundary curve requiring perhaps 100 parameters for complete specification, achieving dramatic dimensionality reduction while preserving the information most relevant for clinical decision-making.

The key insight is that many clinically important applications are fundamentally segmentation tasks rather than continuous field estimation problems. Tumor detection asks "Is there a stiff nodule present? Where is it located? How large is it?" These questions concern geometric localization—shape, size, and position—not precise Young's modulus quantification at every spatial point. Similarly, tissue engineering assessment seeks to identify which scaffold regions have been remodeled by cells into stiffer tissue, and defect localization in manufactured parts aims to find voids or delaminations. In each case, the inclusion-background distinction constitutes the diagnostic feature, making binary segmentation the appropriate problem formulation.

This reformulation enables leveraging U-Net's proven strength in medical image segmentation. Originally developed by Ronneberger and colleagues for cell detection and boundary delineation in microscopy images, U-Net has become the architecture of choice for pixel-wise classification tasks in biomedical imaging. By framing stiffness reconstruction as segmentation rather than regression, we access this mature body of knowledge and battle-tested architectures while dramatically simplifying the inverse problem through dimensionality reduction.

The distinction from Goenezen's mechanics-based tomography deserves emphasis, as this represents the closest prior work and will certainly draw comparison. Goenezen solves for continuous E(x,y) at every element using iterative adjoint optimization—a general formulation applicable to arbitrary material heterogeneity. PAT-Scan reframes the problem specifically for two-component systems as geometric segmentation, reducing unknowns from element-wise properties to boundary curve parameters. Goenezen's approach proves computationally intensive with risk of local minima during optimization; our learned U-Net mapping achieves fast single-forward-pass inference after training. The scope differs as well: Goenezen demonstrated feasibility for the general inverse problem, while PAT-Scan adds systematic angular scanning protocols and universal geometry handling that functions across circular, elliptical, and irregular shapes without modification.

When should one use the geometric inverse formulation versus the full continuous inverse problem? The answer depends on the physical system. For discrete pathologies—tumor nodules with distinct boundaries, manufactured defects with sharp edges, tissue-engineered regions with defined interfaces—geometric segmentation proves appropriate and computationally efficient. For gradual spatial variations—fibrosis progression showing smooth stiffness gradients, compositionally-graded engineered materials, continuously heterogeneous geological formations—the full inverse problem addressed in Aim 2b becomes necessary. PAT-Scan's contribution is recognizing when reformulation to the geometric problem suffices and exploiting that structure for computational advantage.

### Innovation 2: Mesh-Based Physics-Informed Neural Network Architecture

The term "physics-informed neural network" encompasses two distinct computational paradigms that differ fundamentally in how they couple physics constraints with neural network learning. Clarifying this distinction is essential for understanding PAT-Scan's methodological contribution and positioning the work appropriately within the rapidly evolving PINN literature.

Meshfree physics-informed neural networks, as developed by Raissi, Karniadakis, and colleagues, embed partial differential equations directly into the neural network training loss through automatic differentiation. The network learns to approximate the solution field while simultaneously satisfying governing equations, constitutive relations, and boundary conditions at collocation points sampled throughout the domain. The loss function combines data-driven terms (matching measurements where available) with physics-driven terms penalizing PDE residuals: L = L_data + λ·L_PDE, where L_PDE = ||∇·σ + f||² enforces equilibrium and similar terms enforce strain-displacement compatibility and stress-strain constitutive relations. This approach elegantly handles complex geometries without traditional discretization and has achieved impressive results on forward and inverse problems across fluid mechanics, solid mechanics, and heat transfer.

However, meshfree PINNs face computational challenges when applied to inverse problems in structural mechanics. The network must simultaneously solve the forward PDE (satisfying equilibrium) and identify unknown material parameters—a coupled optimization that can prove expensive and difficult to converge. Each training iteration requires computing PDE residuals through automatic differentiation of the network outputs with respect to spatial coordinates, adding substantial computational overhead compared to pure data-driven learning. Physical accuracy is approximate, depending on how well the loss minimization succeeds in reducing PDE residuals at collocation points; typical residuals achieve magnitudes of 10^-3 to 10^-5 rather than the 10^-12 precision available from direct solvers.

Our mesh-based PINN approach follows a different philosophy: decouple the forward and inverse problems, leveraging the strengths of each computational method for its appropriate task. The forward model uses traditional finite element analysis to solve the well-posed linear elasticity problem F = KU. Forces F are applied at specified boundary nodes, the stiffness matrix K is assembled from known element properties through standard procedures, and displacements U are computed via direct sparse Cholesky decomposition. This FEM component represents established computational mechanics—highly efficient through sparse linear algebra, guaranteed to satisfy equilibrium equations to numerical precision (10^-12 relative error in double precision), and requiring only seconds of computation time on CPU for typical mesh sizes of 800 nodes and 1,500 elements.

The inverse model employs a U-Net neural network to learn the ill-posed mapping from measured boundary displacements to material property fields. The network processes displacement data as a two-channel image (x-displacement and y-displacement fields interpolated onto a regular 64×64 grid) and produces a one-channel output (material property map on the same grid). Training proceeds without explicit PDE residuals in the loss function. Instead, physics is encoded implicitly through the FEM-generated training data, which inherently satisfies mechanical equilibrium, and explicitly through Total Variation regularization that preserves sharp material interfaces characteristic of piecewise-constant elasticity distributions.

This decoupled architecture offers several concrete advantages. Computational efficiency improves dramatically because finite element forward solves leverage sparse linear algebra (O(N^1.5) complexity for N degrees of freedom with modern solvers) rather than backpropagating through PDE residuals (O(N_collocation × N_parameters) evaluations at each training iteration). In practice, this translates to FEM solution times of seconds on CPU versus minutes to hours for meshfree PINN training on GPU for problems of comparable size—a 10 to 100 times speedup that compounds over the thousands of forward solves needed to generate training datasets.

Physical guarantees strengthen because the forward model exactly satisfies equilibrium equations within numerical precision, whereas meshfree PINNs satisfy equilibrium only approximately depending on loss minimization success. For applications where mechanical equilibrium must hold strictly—structural analysis, safety-critical systems, validation against experimental data—this exactness proves valuable. The residual is not approximately minimized but rather driven to machine precision through direct linear algebra.

Modularity enables independent improvement of either component without restructuring the entire framework. Advances in finite element methods—adaptive mesh refinement, nonlinear material models, contact mechanics—can be incorporated into the forward solver without modifying the neural network architecture. Conversely, advances in deep learning—transformer architectures, diffusion models, improved segmentation networks—can be substituted for U-Net without changing the FEM component. This separation of concerns facilitates incremental development and incorporation of future innovations.

Recent work corroborates this hybrid approach. Wu and colleagues developed JAX-SSO in 2024, demonstrating that differentiable finite element analysis combined with neural networks enables efficient structural optimization with seamless physics integration. Xue and coworkers created JAX-FEM in 2023, a GPU-accelerated differentiable 3D finite element solver specifically designed for automatic inverse design and mechanistic data science applications. These efforts recognize the value in combining classical discretization methods with modern differentiable programming, situating mesh-based PINNs as a distinct and growing subfield within physics-informed machine learning.

Our implementation includes an option for fully differentiable physics-informed training through the `unet_forward_model_differentiable.py` module. This component assembles the FEM stiffness matrix from predicted material properties, solves for predicted displacements, and computes residuals against measured displacements for inclusion in the loss function: L_total = L_data + λ_physics·||U_predicted - U_measured||². This enables physics-informed training when desired while maintaining the flexibility for purely data-driven learning when the implicit physics encoding through FEM-generated data suffices. The framework accommodates both paradigms.

This decoupling should be framed as an intentional design choice rather than a limitation or compromise. We are not "settling for mesh-based because meshfree is too difficult." Rather, we are strategically separating the well-posed forward problem (best handled by mature, efficient FEM solvers) from the ill-posed inverse problem (where neural networks excel at learning complex mappings). This separation exploits the best capabilities of each computational approach, achieving efficiency and rigor simultaneously.

### Innovation 3: CT-Inspired Angular Scanning with Universal Geometry Handling

The measurement strategy in PAT-Scan draws explicit inspiration from computed tomography's fundamental principle: interrogating the sample from multiple angles reveals interior structure from boundary projections. Rather than applying a single force configuration and attempting reconstruction from limited data, we systematically apply force pairs at varying angular positions around the boundary, collecting displacement measurements after each loading. This sequential scanning protocol builds directly on the multiple loading insight pioneered by Konofagou and Harrigan's palpation tomography work.

Their key contribution demonstrated that applying multiple distinct loads rather than a single large-area compression "increased the ratio of measurements to fitted parameters, which made the method less sensitive to random errors." The palpation tomography experiments achieved noise reduction by a factor of two using nine loading cases compared to single-load elastography. This work established the principle that measurement diversity improves inverse problem conditioning. PAT-Scan extends this concept through systematic angular scanning, sweeping from 1 to 20 force pair configurations with controlled angular spacing.

Our angular scanning protocol reveals progressive information accumulation as force pair count increases. With a single force pair applied at opposing boundary points (0° and 180°), displacement concentrates near the force application locations, providing limited interrogation of the inclusion's mechanical influence. Adding force pairs at 90° and 270° increases boundary coverage and creates a more complex deformation pattern. As the number of force pairs grows to 10 and eventually 20, deformation becomes increasingly uniform around the boundary, with the displacement pattern encoding richer information about the inclusion's shape, size, position, and stiffness. Animation of the scanning process makes this evolution visually apparent: sparse angular sampling yields localized deformation, while dense angular sampling produces near-complete boundary interrogation reminiscent of CT's many-angle projections enabling tomographic reconstruction.

A critical finding emerged during development that has important implications for experimental system design: boundary displacement completeness matters more than force magnitude variation. Experiments comparing partial boundary coverage (square sample with measurements from a single edge) versus complete boundary coverage (circular sample with full perimeter access) showed dramatic reconstruction accuracy improvements with complete coverage, even when applied forces were smaller in magnitude. This observation suggests that practical measurement systems must enable multi-angle access to the sample boundary through rotating stages that allow sample orientation changes, mirror systems providing simultaneous multi-angle views, or multiple synchronized cameras capturing different boundary regions.

The decision to exclude radial force scanning—varying force magnitude at fixed angular positions—represents an intentional design choice grounded in linear elasticity physics rather than an overlooked opportunity. Force scaling by a constant factor (F → 2F) yields linearly scaled displacement response (U → 2U) without providing new geometric information about the inclusion. Only angular diversity, which changes the spatial pattern of applied forces, creates genuinely new interrogation configurations that constrain the inverse problem. This reasoning, documented in the PhD reflection, justifies angular scanning as the appropriate strategy for quasi-static linear elasticity.

Universal geometry handling constitutes an innovation that deserves more emphasis than it has received in the current literature. The same U-Net architecture functions across circular, elliptical, and irregular inclusion geometries without architectural modifications or geometry-specific parameter tuning. Training scripts automatically detect geometry type from dataset metadata—identifying the presence of radius parameters for circular cases or Fourier coefficients for irregular cases—and proceed with identical network architecture and training procedures. The network learns to recognize geometric features directly from displacement field patterns, adapting its internal representations to handle circular symmetry or irregular complexity as the data demands.

Level-set post-processing complements this universality by extracting boundaries from soft neural network predictions without geometric assumptions. The three-step pipeline—Gaussian smoothing to suppress noise, soft thresholding via sigmoid to sharpen transitions, and contour extraction to identify the 0.5 level set—works for arbitrary topologies. Convex or non-convex shapes, simply connected or multiply connected regions, smooth or spiculated boundaries all yield to the same post-processing procedure because level-set methods make no prior assumptions about boundary structure. This generality proves essential for handling realistic tumor geometries with irregular, spiculated boundaries that defy simple parametric description.

From a practical hardware perspective, the force application strategy requires only simple actuation: calibrated pushers or compressed air jets directed radially inward at specified boundary angles. This proves substantially simpler than the vibration exciters needed for Visual Vibration Tomography or the ultrasound transducers required for ultrasound elastography, supporting the accessibility goal that motivates this work.

### Innovation 4: Synthetic-to-Real Training Pathway

A common criticism of machine learning approaches in computational mechanics centers on data requirements. Clinical tissue measurements are expensive to collect, requiring patient recruitment, imaging protocol development, regulatory approval, and specialized equipment operation. Annotating such data with ground truth material properties proves even more challenging—how does one know the true Young's modulus distribution when that is precisely what we seek to measure?

PAT-Scan circumvents this data bottleneck by using the finite element forward model as a synthetic data generator. The FEM solver accepts arbitrary geometric descriptions and material property distributions, solves the linear elasticity equations to compute displacement fields under specified loading, and outputs perfectly labeled training pairs: displacement input matched with material property target. This enables unlimited data generation with controlled parameter sweeps across stiffness contrasts (2:1 to 20:1 ratios), inclusion sizes (0.2 to 0.4 times the outer radius), shapes (circular, elliptical, irregular with varying Fourier perturbation amplitudes), loading configurations (1 to 20 force pairs at varied angular positions), and noise levels (0.1% to 5% additive Gaussian simulating Digital Image Correlation measurement uncertainty).

This synthetic training approach is defensible for proof-of-concept development because the forward model accurately represents the underlying physics of quasi-static tissue deformation. Linear elasticity with Hooke's law constitutive relations has been validated experimentally across countless materials and loading conditions. The equilibrium equations ∇·σ = 0 derive from fundamental force balance and are not empirical approximations. Finite element discretization has mature mathematical foundations with well-understood convergence properties. Therefore, synthetic FEM data captures the essential physical relationships that the neural network must learn—the mapping from applied forces and material distributions to resulting displacement fields—with fidelity limited only by the small-deformation linear elasticity assumptions rather than by simulation artifacts.

The pathway from synthetic to real validation follows a staged strategy that progressively incorporates experimental complexity. First, training and testing occur entirely on FEM-generated data to establish that the inverse solver works in principle under ideal conditions. This stage—currently complete for circular geometries and in progress for irregular shapes—demonstrates algorithmic feasibility without experimental overhead. Second, silicone tissue-mimicking phantoms with known stiffness values provide intermediate validation in controlled laboratory conditions. Real measurement noise from Digital Image Correlation and force sensors enters the system, but geometric ground truth remains available through direct measurement and material properties through separate mechanical testing. Third, ex-vivo tissue samples introduce biological variability while maintaining some level of ground truth through mechanical testing of excised specimens and comparison to anatomical features. Fourth, and most speculatively, clinical measurements on patients would provide ultimate validation against the actual diagnostic context, though this lies well beyond the current PhD scope.

Transfer learning bridges the synthetic-to-real gap efficiently. A neural network pre-trained on millions of synthetic examples develops internal representations of displacement-to-stiffness mappings and geometric feature extraction. Fine-tuning on a small dataset of real measurements adapts the network to measurement-specific noise characteristics, systematic biases in the imaging system, and geometric variations not fully captured in simulation. This data-efficient adaptation strategy has proven successful across computer vision, where ImageNet pre-training followed by fine-tuning on small task-specific datasets has become standard practice. The same principle applies to mechanics inverse problems: learn general physics-based mappings from unlimited synthetic data, then adapt to experimental reality with limited real data.

## Specific Aim 1: Proof-of-Concept for Geometric Inverse Problem

### Problem Formulation

Aim 1 addresses the geometric inverse problem: recovering the shape, size, and location of a stiff inclusion embedded in a soft background material when displacement measurements are available only at the boundary. The material distribution is assumed piecewise constant—two distinct Young's modulus values (E_background and E_inclusion) separated by a sharp interface. This binary material assumption models clinically relevant scenarios where a stiff tumor nodule is embedded in compliant surrounding tissue, or where a manufactured defect creates a discrete region of altered mechanical properties within an otherwise homogeneous structure.

This formulation differs fundamentally from the full inverse problem where material properties vary continuously in space as E(x,y). The geometric inverse problem is more tractable because it reduces the unknown from a continuous field requiring thousands of parameters to a finite set of geometric parameters describing the inclusion boundary curve. However, recovering this boundary from boundary-only displacement measurements remains challenging due to the ill-posed nature of all elasticity inverse problems. The mathematical non-uniqueness—multiple material distributions can produce nearly identical boundary displacements—combines with measurement noise sensitivity to demand careful algorithm design and regularization.

The canonical test case employs a circular domain with outer radius R_outer = 1.0 containing a concentric circular inclusion of radius R_inner = 0.3. Material properties are assigned as E_background = 1.0 and E_inclusion = 10.0, representing a 10:1 stiffness contrast typical of soft tissue-tumor systems such as breast cancer. Poisson's ratio is set to ν = 0.3 for both materials, and all nodes within the inclusion boundary are fixed at zero displacement to mimic a rigid core that cannot deform under the applied boundary forces.

### Aim 1a: FEM Forward Model Development

Finite element modeling provides the computational foundation for both training data generation and physics validation. The mesh generation strategy employs a structured polar grid, dividing the circular domain into 20 radial layers and 40 angular segments. This structured approach ensures consistent element quality across the domain and simplifies material assignment through geometric tests based on radial distance from the origin. The resulting mesh contains approximately 800 nodes and 1,500 triangular elements, providing sufficient spatial resolution to capture displacement gradients while maintaining computational efficiency for the thousands of forward solves needed during dataset generation.

The finite element formulation employs plane stress assumption, appropriate for thin samples where out-of-plane stresses are negligible compared to in-plane components. Each triangular element contributes a 6×6 stiffness matrix relating the displacements of its three nodes (two degrees of freedom per node) to the forces acting on those nodes. These element matrices are computed from the element geometry and assigned material properties following standard isoparametric finite element procedures. Assembly into the global sparse stiffness matrix K of dimension 2N_nodes × 2N_nodes proceeds through the standard direct stiffness method, accumulating element contributions into the appropriate rows and columns determined by node connectivity.

Force application follows a paired strategy designed to prevent rigid body motion while creating informative deformation patterns. Equal and opposite radial forces are applied at boundary nodes located at angle θ and θ + π, pushing inward toward the inclusion. This balanced loading ensures force equilibrium and moment equilibrium, eliminating the need for additional constraints to suppress rigid body modes. The magnitude of applied forces is constrained by a geometric validity requirement: the deformed boundary must not penetrate the fixed inclusion boundary. A penetration checking function evaluates the minimum distance between deformed boundary nodes and the inclusion after solving for displacements, determining the maximum allowable force magnitude for each angular configuration.

Solution of the resulting linear system KU = F proceeds through direct sparse linear algebra. Boundary conditions are enforced by modifying rows of the stiffness matrix K and force vector F to impose zero displacement at all nodes within the inclusion. The sparse Cholesky decomposition solver then computes the displacement vector U with numerical precision limited only by floating-point arithmetic—typically achieving relative errors near 10^-12 in double precision. Solution times remain on the order of seconds even for the largest meshes considered (approximately 1,600 nodes and 3,000 elements), confirming that the forward model is not a computational bottleneck for the overall inverse solver framework.

Validation of the finite element implementation confirmed several expected physical behaviors. Displacement fields achieve maximum values at force application points and decay with increasing distance from these locations, matching physical intuition for elastic deformation under localized loading. The stiff inclusion experiences minimal deformation while the soft background deforms substantially, correctly reflecting the 10:1 stiffness contrast. Automated testing scripts systematically sweep force magnitudes and angular configurations, verifying solution stability and penetration constraint satisfaction across the entire operating range without numerical failures or unphysical results.

### Aim 1b: Dataset Generation via Angular Scanning

The angular scanning protocol systematically explores the space of force configurations to generate training data with varying richness of boundary interrogation. Beginning from a single force pair applied at angles 0° and 180°, the number of force pairs is incrementally increased up to 20, with angular spacing computed as 360°/N_pairs to ensure uniform coverage around the boundary. For each configuration, the finite element solver computes the equilibrium displacement field satisfying force balance and constitutive relations, and displacements at boundary nodes are extracted as the raw measurement data.

Converting these irregular boundary node positions to the regular grid format required for neural network training demands interpolation. Displacement components are interpolated onto a 64×64 pixel grid covering the square domain [-1.2, 1.2] × [-1.2, 1.2], creating two-channel images representing x-displacement and y-displacement fields. The ground truth target for supervised learning is a binary material mask on the same grid, with pixels labeled 1 inside the inclusion boundary and 0 outside. This representation transforms the mechanical inverse problem into an image segmentation task, enabling the use of convolutional neural networks optimized for spatial reasoning.

The scanning process generates 20 training samples per geometric configuration, each representing a distinct level of angular interrogation richness. A dataset saved to disk includes the complete force vectors applied for each sample, the number of force pairs, clearance distances between the deformed boundary and inclusion, and maximum displacement magnitudes. This metadata enables later analysis of how reconstruction accuracy depends on interrogation completeness—a relationship that proves important for understanding the limits of boundary-only measurements.

Animation of the scanning process provides visual insight into how deformation patterns evolve with increasing force pair count. With a single pair, displacement localizes near the force application points with minimal response on the opposite side of the domain. As additional pairs are added at 90°, 180°, and 270°, the deformation pattern becomes more symmetric and provides richer sampling of the inclusion's mechanical influence on boundary response. By 20 force pairs, deformation appears nearly uniform around the boundary, with subtle asymmetries encoding information about the inclusion's presence and properties. This progressive information accumulation mirrors computed tomography's principle of improving reconstruction quality through multiple projection angles.

### Aim 1c: U-Net Training and Boundary Extraction

The neural network architecture follows the U-Net design originally developed for biomedical image segmentation, which proves well-suited for this geometric inverse problem. The network accepts as input the two-channel displacement field (64×64 pixels with Ux and Uy components) and produces as output a one-channel material property field on the same 64×64 grid. The encoder pathway consists of three levels of convolution-ReLU activation-max pooling operations, progressively downsampling from 64×64 to 32×32 to 16×16 spatial resolution while increasing feature channels from the base 32 to 64 to 128. This downsampling captures increasingly abstract semantic information about displacement patterns and their relationship to underlying material distributions.

The decoder pathway reverses this process through three levels of transposed convolution operations, upsampling from 16×16 back to 64×64 while decreasing feature channels from 128 to 64 to 32 and finally to 1 for the output material map. Critically, skip connections link each encoder level to its corresponding decoder level, concatenating feature maps before the decoder's convolution operations. These connections enable the network to combine high-level semantic understanding from the encoder bottleneck with fine-grained spatial information from earlier encoder stages, which proves essential for accurate boundary localization in segmentation tasks.

The training loss function combines mean squared error with Total Variation regularization: L = ||E_pred - E_true||² + λ_TV × Σ|∇E_pred|. The MSE term provides standard supervised learning, penalizing pixel-wise differences between predicted and ground truth material fields. The Total Variation term penalizes the magnitude of spatial gradients throughout the predicted field, implementing a strong preference for piecewise constant solutions. This regularization proves crucial for the geometric inverse problem because the expected solution is indeed piecewise constant—sharp transitions between inclusion and background with uniform values within each region.

The connection between Total Variation regularization and the subsequent level-set post-processing deserves emphasis. Without the TV penalty, neural networks tend to produce soft, gradually varying transitions between material regions. While mean squared error alone might achieve numerically low loss values, the resulting smooth fields lack the sharp 0/1 separation needed for clean boundary extraction. The soft thresholding step that converts continuous predictions to discrete boundaries becomes arbitrary and unstable when applied to gradual transitions. Total Variation regularization guides the network toward producing naturally sharp transitions, pre-conditioning the output for robust level-set extraction. The TV-regularized prediction exhibits rapid changes across narrow transition zones, making the 0.5 contour a natural and well-defined choice for the material interface.

Hyperparameter optimization proceeded through systematic grid search over learning rate (10^-5, 10^-4, 10^-3), TV weight λ_TV (0.001, 0.005, 0.01), and thresholding temperature T (2000, 3000, 5000). Each configuration was trained for 5,000 iterations using the Adam optimizer, and performance was evaluated on held-out validation geometries not seen during training. Results indicated that learning rate 10^-4, TV weight 0.005, and temperature 3000 provided the most consistent reconstruction quality across different stiffness contrasts and geometric configurations. These hyperparameters were adopted for all subsequent training.

The post-processing pipeline transforms soft neural network predictions into hard geometric boundaries through three sequential steps. First, Gaussian smoothing with standard deviation σ ≈ 0.03 (in grid units) suppresses high-frequency noise and further regularizes the predicted field. Second, soft thresholding via the sigmoid function E_thresh = sigmoid(T × (E_smooth - 0.5)) sharpens the transition between materials, with the temperature parameter T controlling steepness. Higher temperatures produce sharper, more step-like transitions approaching a hard threshold; lower temperatures yield gradual soft transitions. Third, contour extraction identifies the 0.5 level set of the thresholded field, yielding a polygon or spline representation of the inclusion boundary that can be compared to ground truth and used for quantitative accuracy metrics.

Training convergence was monitored through loss curves and visual inspection of predictions on validation samples. The combined loss typically decreased from initial values around 0.1 to final values near 0.01 over 5,000 iterations, representing an order of magnitude improvement. Animation of training evolution shows the network initially predicting a nearly uniform material field with little spatial structure, then gradually developing a circular feature that progressively sharpens and localizes to match the ground truth inclusion. By iteration 5,000, the predicted inclusion boundary closely tracks the true boundary, with typical position errors below 5% of the inclusion radius.

Reconstruction accuracy has been evaluated qualitatively through visual comparison of predicted and true boundaries overlaid on the same plots. The level-set extraction successfully identifies sharp inclusion boundaries from the soft U-Net outputs across all test cases examined. Predicted inclusion shapes closely match the ground truth circles in both size and position. However, quantitative metrics including Dice coefficient (measuring spatial overlap between predicted and true regions), Hausdorff distance (measuring maximum boundary error), and radius estimation error remain to be computed from existing checkpoint files. The current status is best characterized as qualitative validation demonstrating proof-of-concept functionality, with rigorous quantitative benchmarking as ongoing work to be completed.

One consistent observation across all reconstructions warrants discussion: the recovered stiffness value within the inclusion tends to be 60-80% of the target value rather than exactly matching the assigned E_inclusion = 10.0. This systematic underestimation likely reflects fundamental physics of the boundary-only inverse problem rather than a correctable algorithmic deficiency. Displacement measurements at the boundary strongly constrain geometric parameters—where is the inclusion, how large is it, what shape does it have—because these directly affect the spatial pattern of boundary response. However, absolute stiffness magnitude proves more weakly constrained because a stiffer inclusion of smaller size can produce boundary displacements similar to a softer inclusion of larger size. Total Variation regularization, which biases solutions toward piecewise constant fields with minimal gradient, may further dampen peak stiffness values by preferring smoother transitions.

For clinical tumor detection applications, this underestimation may prove acceptable or even irrelevant. The diagnostic questions "Is there a tumor? Where is it? How big is it?" concern geometric localization rather than precise Young's modulus quantification. Relative stiffness contrast between tumor and background matters more than absolute values, particularly given that tissue-to-tissue variability in mechanical properties can easily reach ±30% even within the same tissue type. Accurate geometric boundary recovery enables surgical planning and biopsy targeting even when absolute stiffness values are underestimated by a consistent factor.

### Discussion: Aim 1 Achievements and Limitations

Aim 1 successfully demonstrated proof-of-concept for the geometric inverse problem applied to centered circular inclusions. The integrated pipeline—finite element forward model, angular scanning dataset generation, U-Net training with Total Variation regularization, and level-set boundary extraction—functions as designed and produces physically plausible reconstructions that qualitatively match ground truth geometric features.

Key achievements include establishing the mesh-based PINN framework architecture with clear separation between physics-based forward solving and learned inverse mapping. The approach validates that boundary-only displacement measurements contain sufficient information for inclusion reconstruction without requiring full-field interior measurements, dramatically simplifying hardware requirements compared to methods needing ultrasound or MRI imaging throughout the volume. Total Variation regularization proves effective for geometric inverse problems, producing naturally sharp material transitions that enable robust level-set extraction. Hyperparameter optimization through systematic grid search identified training configurations that generalize across different stiffness contrasts, suggesting the learned mappings capture physics rather than memorizing specific examples.

The work also revealed important limitations and scope boundaries that define future research directions. The two-dimensional plane stress assumption limits current applicability to thin samples, though three-dimensional extension addressed in Aim 3a represents a natural generalization rather than a fundamental barrier. Restriction to centered circular inclusions constitutes an artificial simplification adopted for proof-of-concept clarity; irregular geometry support in Aim 2a relaxes this constraint. All validation currently occurs on synthetic finite element data without exposure to real measurement noise, biological variability, or hardware-specific systematic errors—experimental validation with physical phantoms in Aim 3c will provide the critical test of whether synthetic training transfers to real-world measurements.

Boundary displacement completeness emerged as a critical requirement with important implications for experimental system design. Experiments with partial boundary coverage (square sample with measurements from only one edge) showed dramatically degraded reconstruction accuracy compared to complete coverage (circular sample with measurements around the full perimeter), even when the completeness case used smaller applied forces. This finding indicates that practical measurement systems must provide multi-angle access to most or all of the sample boundary, potentially requiring rotating stages to reorient the sample, mirror systems to provide simultaneous views from multiple angles, or synchronized camera arrays capturing different boundary regions. Single-view, single-edge measurement configurations will not suffice for accurate reconstruction.

The current status is most accurately characterized as "core methodology demonstrated" rather than "fully completed and validated." The algorithmic framework is functional and produces correct results on canonical test cases, establishing feasibility. However, quantitative performance metrics (Dice coefficient, Hausdorff distance, statistical analysis over many test samples) remain to be computed from existing checkpoint data, and broader geometric variation testing awaits completion of Aim 2a. The foundation is solid; the comprehensive validation continues.

## Specific Aim 2: Extension to Irregular Geometries and Advanced Architectures

### Overall Goal and Status

Aim 2 extends PAT-Scan beyond the proof-of-concept canonical circular inclusions to handle realistic geometric complexity and explore alternative neural architectures for the full inverse problem. This aim subdivides into three components with substantially different completion levels. Aim 2a, addressing irregular off-centered inclusions, is approximately 60% complete with geometry generation, finite element solver adaptation, and universal training framework all implemented and functional, though quantitative benchmarking across systematic geometric variations remains incomplete. Aim 2b, proposing Fourier feature multilayer perceptrons for continuous material field reconstruction, is planned but not yet implemented, representing future work for later research phases. Aim 2c, developing a comprehensive benchmarking library with 20-50 diverse test cases, is similarly planned but not implemented.

### Aim 2a: Irregular Off-Centered Inclusions

Real tumors exhibit geometric features that challenge reconstruction algorithms: spiculated boundaries with sharp projections, non-convex shapes with indentations and irregular lobes, and off-centered positions that break the convenient symmetries of the canonical circular case. Aim 2a introduces this complexity through Fourier mode perturbations applied to a circular base shape, creating inclusions whose boundary radius varies with angle according to r(θ) = R_base × (1 + Σ[a_n cos(nθ) + b_n sin(nθ)]). The number of Fourier modes N_modes typically equals 6, and the Fourier coefficients {a_n, b_n} are randomly sampled within prescribed bounds to generate diverse irregular shapes. An irregularity parameter controls the perturbation amplitude, and clamping constraints ensure that r(θ) remains between 0.5 and 1.5 times the base radius, preventing self-intersection and extreme aspect ratios that would yield invalid geometries.

Additionally, the inclusion center can be displaced from the domain center by offsets (c_x, c_y) ranging from -0.5 to 0.5 in normalized coordinates. This off-centering creates fundamentally asymmetric deformation patterns under loading, providing a more demanding test of the reconstruction algorithm's ability to handle geometric complexity without relying on symmetry assumptions. The combination of Fourier-perturbed boundaries and off-centered positioning generates inclusion shapes that begin to approximate the spiculated, irregular morphology of real tumors observed in clinical imaging.

Mesh generation for irregular geometries required generalizing the material assignment logic from the simple radial distance threshold used for circular inclusions. The algorithm transforms each element centroid to local coordinates centered on the inclusion, computes the angular position θ, evaluates the Fourier series to determine the boundary radius r_boundary(θ) at that specific angle, and compares the element's distance from the inclusion center to this angle-dependent threshold. Elements within the boundary are assigned to the inclusion; those outside are assigned to the background. This generalization handles arbitrary Fourier-generated boundaries without modification to the underlying approach.

Remarkably, the finite element solver itself required no changes to accommodate irregular geometries. The same linear elasticity formulation, element stiffness computation, global assembly procedure, boundary condition enforcement, and sparse direct solution apply regardless of inclusion shape. Geometric complexity is entirely absorbed into the mesh generation stage, leaving the solver untouched. This modularity validates a key design principle of the decoupled mesh-based PINN architecture: physics solving and geometric representation remain independent, with complexity managed through appropriate abstraction layers.

The training framework incorporates automatic geometry detection by inspecting dataset metadata fields. If the dataset contains fields named 'a_coeffs' and 'b_coeffs' storing the Fourier coefficients, the script enters irregular geometry mode; otherwise it defaults to circular mode. The same U-Net architecture employed for circular cases functions without modification on irregular cases—no architectural changes, no geometry-specific hyperparameters, no algorithm adjustments. The network learns to extract geometric features directly from displacement field patterns, adapting its internal representations to handle simple circular symmetry or complex irregular boundaries as the training data demands.

Preliminary training results on irregular geometries show promise while revealing increased sensitivity to hyperparameters compared to circular cases. The optimal Total Variation weight and smoothing parameters differ somewhat from values that worked well for circles, suggesting that irregular boundaries with higher-frequency geometric features require more careful regularization tuning. Level-set extraction handles non-convex shapes successfully, demonstrating that the post-processing pipeline generalizes beyond simple circular topology to capture indentations, protrusions, and boundary irregularities without geometric assumptions.

Future work to complete Aim 2a includes systematic training across 10-20 irregular samples spanning a range of Fourier mode amplitudes from subtle perturbations to highly irregular boundaries, computing quantitative accuracy metrics (Dice coefficient for irregular shape overlap, Hausdorff distance for boundary error, centroid localization error) using the same metrics planned for circular cases, and investigating transfer learning: does a network trained on circular geometries generalize to irregular geometries without retraining, and vice versa? The hypothesis is that training on diverse geometries will produce a more robust inverse solver than training exclusively on symmetric circular cases, though this requires experimental confirmation.

### Aim 2b: Fourier Features MLP for Full Inverse Problem (Planned)

While Aims 1 and 2a address the geometric inverse problem with piecewise constant material properties, many applications require reconstructing continuous spatially-varying material fields E(x,y). Fibrosis progression shows gradual stiffness increases rather than sharp boundaries. Compositionally graded engineered materials exhibit smoothly varying properties by design. Geological formations have heterogeneity at multiple length scales without discrete interfaces. For these problems, the geometric segmentation approach proves insufficient; the full inverse problem must be solved.

Implicit neural representations offer an architectural alternative to grid-based U-Net for continuous field reconstruction. Rather than discretizing space onto a fixed grid and learning material properties at each grid point, implicit representations parameterize a continuous function E(x,y) that can be queried at arbitrary spatial locations. The network maps input coordinates (x,y) to output Young's modulus E, enabling resolution-independent querying that adapts naturally to regions requiring fine or coarse spatial sampling.

Fourier feature mapping addresses a known limitation of standard multilayer perceptrons with ReLU activations: difficulty learning high-frequency spatial variations. Tancik and colleagues demonstrated that embedding input coordinates as γ(p) = [cos(2πB·p), sin(2πB·p)] where B is a random frequency matrix sampled at network initialization, enables MLPs to learn high-frequency functions that would otherwise require prohibitively deep networks or fail entirely. This simple preprocessing step maps the low-dimensional input (x,y) to a high-dimensional frequency space where linear combinations of periodic basis functions can represent complex spatial patterns.

The proposed architecture accepts four inputs for each query point: x-coordinate, y-coordinate, x-displacement u_x(x,y), and y-displacement u_y(x,y). Fourier feature embedding expands these to perhaps 256 or 512 dimensions through the periodic encoding, then a multilayer perceptron with 6-8 hidden layers and 256 hidden units per layer maps to a single scalar output: Young's modulus E at the query location. Training proceeds by sampling random query points (x,y) throughout the domain, extracting displacements at those locations through interpolation if necessary, and minimizing the error between predicted and true E values.

Training data generation requires creating synthetic finite element samples with continuous E(x,y) distributions rather than the piecewise constant fields used in Aims 1-2a. Example distributions include multiple inclusions with different stiffness values and smooth transitions between them, radial gradients E(r) = E_0 + k×r representing property variation with distance from center, Perlin noise-based heterogeneity creating smooth random fields with controlled correlation length, and Gaussian process samples parameterized by covariance functions. For each synthetic sample, the finite element solver assigns spatially varying element properties according to the specified E(x,y) field, solves for displacements, and outputs (x, y, u_x, u_y, E) tuples for training the implicit representation.

The loss function could incorporate both data-driven and physics-informed components: L = ||E_pred(x,y) - E_true(x,y)||² + λ_physics × (displacement prediction error). The physics term would use the differentiable finite element module to assemble the stiffness matrix from the predicted E-field sampled at element centroids, solve for predicted displacements throughout the domain, and penalize deviation from measured displacements. This ensures the predicted material field is not only numerically close to ground truth but also physically consistent with observed mechanical behavior under the applied loading.

Expected outcomes include demonstrating feasibility of continuous E(x,y) reconstruction from boundary displacement measurements, which remains uncertain given the increased ill-posedness compared to the geometric problem; benchmarking the multilayer perceptron approach against U-Net on the geometric inverse problem as a controlled comparison to assess representational capacity; and quantifying the resolution independence advantage of implicit representations, determining whether the ability to query at arbitrary points provides practical benefits over fixed-grid discretization.

Anticipated challenges include substantially longer training times for MLPs compared to convolutional networks on image-like data, even with Fourier features to accelerate convergence; potential requirements for larger training datasets due to the increased parameter count and the more underdetermined nature of continuous field reconstruction; and more complex regularization strategies to prevent overfitting to noise or finding physically implausible solutions that happen to match boundary displacements.

### Aim 2c: Benchmarking Library (Planned)

Systematic validation requires diverse test cases that span the range of geometric and material complexity encountered in real applications. The proposed benchmarking library would include 20-50 samples covering circular inclusions with radii varying from 0.2 to 0.4 times the outer radius, elliptical inclusions with aspect ratios from 1.5 to 3.0 and varied orientation angles, irregular Fourier-perturbed inclusions with mode counts ranging from 3 to 9 and perturbation amplitudes from subtle to extreme, multiple-inclusion configurations with 2-3 separate stiff regions testing the algorithm's ability to distinguish distinct features, and biologically-inspired geometries derived from actual tumor boundary segmentations in medical images.

Stiffness contrast would be systematically varied across clinically relevant ranges: E_inclusion/E_background ∈ {2, 5, 10, 20}, corresponding to literature values for tissue pathologies including liver tumors (approximately 2-5 times stiffer), breast cancer (5-10 times stiffer), and fibrotic tissue (2-3 times stiffer). Noise robustness testing would add Gaussian noise to synthetic displacement measurements at levels 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 5% of maximum displacement magnitude, corresponding to the range of Digital Image Correlation measurement uncertainty under various conditions.

Performance metrics would assess both geometric accuracy through Dice coefficient quantifying spatial overlap between predicted and true inclusion regions and Hausdorff distance measuring maximum boundary position error, and material recovery accuracy through L² error in the Young's modulus field and mean absolute percentage error averaged over all elements or query points. Statistical analysis would report mean and standard deviation for each metric across the test set, providing confidence intervals and enabling rigorous comparison to literature baselines such as Goenezen's mechanics-based tomography results showing 22-50% relative L² error.

The benchmark dataset would be published for community use, enabling other researchers to validate alternative inverse elastography methods on standardized test cases. This would facilitate fair comparison between approaches that currently are difficult to assess relative to each other because they use different test problems, different noise models, and different accuracy metrics. Establishing a common benchmark in computational mechanics inverse problems would provide value analogous to MNIST and CIFAR-10 in computer vision or the various shared tasks in natural language processing.

## Specific Aim 3: Incorporating Realism and Experimental Validation (Future Work)

Aim 3 represents the transition from computational proof-of-concept to experimental and clinical feasibility. This aim is planned for later stages of the PhD program, likely years 3-4, and is outlined here to provide context for the overall research trajectory and demonstrate that a viable path to experimental validation exists.

### Aim 3a: 3D Extension

Extending the framework to three dimensions requires generalizing the finite element solver to tetrahedral elements and volumetric domains. Mesh generation tools such as TetGen or Gmsh can create unstructured tetrahedral meshes from geometric descriptions provided as surface meshes, signed distance functions, or constructive solid geometry. The three-dimensional element stiffness matrices are 12×12 for standard 4-node tetrahedra with three displacement components per node, and global assembly follows the same direct stiffness method used in two dimensions but with larger sparse matrices reflecting the additional degree of freedom per node and the three-dimensional connectivity.

The computational challenge scales significantly—three-dimensional finite element problems typically cost 10 to 100 times more than equivalent two-dimensional problems due to increased element count and larger matrix systems. Efficient solvers become critical; options include FEniCS for automated finite element assembly with symbolic weak form specification, MFEM for high-performance computing applications with careful attention to parallel efficiency, and JAX-FEM for GPU-accelerated differentiable simulation compatible with neural network training frameworks.

For the neural network, either three-dimensional U-Net architectures with volumetric convolutions or implicit multilayer perceptron representations developed in Aim 2b could be employed. The MLP approach may prove particularly attractive for three dimensions because it avoids the memory explosion associated with volumetric convolutions on fine grids—a 128³ volume with 32 features requires over 8 million parameters at a single layer, while an MLP queries points independently without storing dense volumetric feature maps.

### Aim 3b: CT Integration for Anatomically-Informed Models

Medical imaging provides patient-specific geometry that could constrain PAT-Scan reconstructions. CT scans yield outer boundary geometry after segmentation, which can be imported into finite element meshing pipelines. The Visible Human Project dataset offers anatomically accurate cross-sections at millimeter resolution that could serve as realistic test geometries representing actual tissue structures rather than simplified synthetic shapes.

A key challenge is that CT intensity values in Hounsfield units correlate with tissue density but not directly with mechanical stiffness. A hybrid approach might constrain tissue-type regions identified from CT (muscle, fat, bone) to have mechanical properties within literature-reported ranges while allowing PAT-Scan to refine local stiffness variations within those bounds. This combines anatomical prior knowledge from imaging with mechanical measurement from palpation-like interrogation.

An example application would be forearm cross-section reconstruction, where muscle tissue with E ≈ 10-50 kPa, fat with E ≈ 2-10 kPa, and bone with E ≈ 10-20 GPa that acts as a rigid boundary all appear in the same cross-section. The question becomes whether PAT-Scan can distinguish these tissue types from boundary displacement measurements alone, or whether anatomical priors from CT are essential to resolve the ambiguity in the inverse problem.

### Aim 3c: Experimental Validation Roadmap

The pathway from simulation to experimental validation follows three phases of increasing complexity and biological realism. Phase 1 employs silicone tissue-mimicking phantoms fabricated under controlled laboratory conditions. The background material would be soft silicone such as Ecoflex 00-30 from Smooth-On with Young's modulus approximately 20-40 kPa mimicking soft tissue, while embedded inclusions would use Ecoflex 00-50 or Dragon Skin silicone with modulus 100-200 kPa achieving the 5-10 times stiffness contrast characteristic of breast cancer. Inclusion geometry—circular, elliptical, or irregular—would be created through molding or manual insertion of separately-cast plugs, with dimensions measured using calipers to provide geometric ground truth.

The measurement system combines calibrated force application with Digital Image Correlation displacement measurement. A load cell with 0.1-10 N range and ±0.01 N accuracy, mounted on a linear actuator for controlled displacement, provides reproducible force application at specified boundary locations. Stereo Digital Image Correlation using two Point Grey or equivalent cameras with 50mm lenses captures surface displacement at sub-pixel resolution, typically 0.01-0.05 pixels corresponding to micrometer-scale accuracy in physical units after calibration. A speckle pattern airbrushed onto the phantom surface enables correlation tracking between reference and deformed images. Total hardware cost is estimated at $8,000-10,000: cameras and lenses approximately $5,000, load cell and actuator $1,000, calibration equipment $500, and phantom fabrication materials $500 per sample.

The validation protocol would apply the angular scanning strategy to the physical phantom, capturing stereo images before and after force application at each of 5-10 angular positions around the boundary. Digital Image Correlation processing yields displacement fields, from which boundary node displacements are extracted. The trained PAT-Scan neural network processes these measured displacements to predict inclusion geometry and material properties. Validation compares predicted geometry to the known fabricated dimensions measured with calipers, and predicted stiffness contrast to values determined through separate indentation testing on material samples.

Success metrics would include Dice coefficient exceeding 0.75 for good geometric overlap, center localization error below 15% of inclusion radius, stiffness ratio within a factor of 2 of ground truth, and Hausdorff boundary error below 10% of inclusion size. These represent realistic targets given the ill-posed nature of the problem and expected measurement noise.

Phase 2 introduces biological variability through ex-vivo tissue samples such as porcine muscle with embedded regions of fat or connective tissue, or bovine liver potentially containing fibrotic areas. The advantage is exposure to real tissue mechanical properties and structural heterogeneity; the challenge is that exact spatial distribution of Young's modulus is unknown, making validation relative rather than absolute. Does PAT-Scan correctly identify regions of higher versus lower stiffness? Do predicted stiff regions correspond to anatomical features visible in dissection or imaging?

Phase 3 represents the most speculative component: in-vivo clinical measurements on patients. Applications might include breast tumor detection comparing PAT-Scan predictions to MRE or mammography, or liver fibrosis staging comparing to FibroScan ultrasound-based elastography. This phase would require IRB approval for human subjects research, careful safety protocols for force application to ensure patient comfort and prevent tissue damage, clinical collaboration with radiologists or oncologists to access patients and compare results to clinical gold standards, and potentially regulatory considerations if the system is considered an investigational device.

Equipment requirements for Phase 1 phantom validation are modest compared to developing entirely new clinical imaging modalities: a complete laboratory setup totals $8,000-10,000, representing a 200-fold cost reduction compared to $2 million MRE systems and a 10-fold reduction compared to $100,000 ultrasound elastography. This accessibility supports the fundamental motivation for PAT-Scan and demonstrates that experimental validation is economically feasible within typical research budgets.

Timeline projections place Phase 1 phantom experiments in year 4 of the PhD program following completion of Aims 1 and 2, Phase 2 ex-vivo tissue testing potentially in years 4-5 if time permits and suitable collaborations can be established, and Phase 3 clinical feasibility studies likely beyond the PhD scope as postdoctoral research or early faculty work.

## Assumptions and Scope

### Material Model Assumptions

The current implementation assumes linear elasticity with small deformations governed by Hooke's law constitutive relations σ = E·ε. This approximation is justified when displacements remain below approximately 5% of sample dimensions and strains below 5%, conditions satisfied in many quasi-static palpation scenarios on soft tissues. Gentle compression of breast tissue during manual examination typically induces 2-4% strain, abdominal palpation produces strains below 5% for gentle pressure, and many controlled laboratory experiments easily maintain the small-strain regime. Linear elasticity provides reasonable accuracy in these regimes and has been extensively validated experimentally.

However, real soft tissues exhibit nonlinear stress-strain behavior, particularly at larger deformations where strain energy functions such as Neo-Hookean or Mooney-Rivlin models become necessary. Extending the framework to nonlinear constitutive laws is technically feasible—the finite element solver would require iterative Newton-Raphson solution rather than direct linear solve—but adds computational cost by factors of 10 to 100 depending on nonlinearity severity and convergence requirements. For proof-of-concept demonstration, linear elasticity suffices to establish methodology; clinical translation may demand nonlinear models depending on the specific application and deformation levels involved.

The plane stress assumption restricts current applicability to thin samples where out-of-plane stresses are negligible. This proves appropriate for two-dimensional proof-of-concept work and for certain physical systems such as thin tissue cross-sections or manufactured sheets, but must be relaxed for realistic three-dimensional applications addressed in Aim 3a. Poisson's ratio is assumed known and spatially constant at ν ≈ 0.3-0.49, with soft tissues nearly incompressible due to high water content. The inverse problem in its current formulation solves only for Young's modulus E, not for Poisson ratio; simultaneous identification of both parameters would require additional measurement information such as volumetric strain or constitute an even more severely ill-posed problem.

### Geometric Assumptions

The outer boundary geometry is assumed known from direct measurement, imaging, or by construction in synthetic test cases. For the canonical circular domain used in Aims 1-2a, this is trivially satisfied by definition. For anatomically realistic geometries in Aim 3b, CT or MRI segmentation provides outer boundary surfaces. For experimental phantoms, the outer boundary can be measured with calipers or extracted from photographs. This is generally not a severe restriction for practical applications where the sample or tissue boundary is externally accessible.

Material distribution in Aims 1-2a is restricted to piecewise constant (geometric inverse problem), though Aim 2b explicitly relaxes this to continuous E(x,y) for the full inverse problem. The piecewise constant assumption models discrete pathologies such as tumor nodules, manufactured defects, or tissue-engineered regions with sharp interfaces, while continuous variation models gradual processes like fibrosis progression or compositional grading.

### Measurement Assumptions

Boundary displacement completeness emerged as a critical requirement with major implications for experimental system design. Reconstruction accuracy degrades substantially when only partial boundary data is available—single-edge measurements on square samples performed far worse than full-perimeter measurements on circular samples even when the latter used smaller forces. Practical measurement systems must therefore enable access to a substantial fraction of the boundary, potentially requiring rotating stages to reorient the sample, mirror systems providing simultaneous multi-angle views, or multiple synchronized cameras capturing different boundary regions. Single-view, single-edge measurement configurations will not suffice.

Displacement measurement accuracy is assumed within Digital Image Correlation capabilities, which typically achieve 0.01-0.05 pixel sub-pixel resolution. In physical units this translates to micrometer-scale accuracy for typical camera setups with appropriate magnification. Noise modeling in synthetic experiments uses additive Gaussian noise at 0.1-1% of maximum displacement, corresponding to realistic DIC uncertainty under good conditions. Systematic errors from calibration inaccuracy or lens distortion are not currently modeled but will enter the picture during experimental validation.

Force measurement accuracy is assumed within standard load cell specifications of ±1% for commercial sensors. The inverse problem appears relatively insensitive to small force magnitude errors based on preliminary testing—displacement pattern matters more than absolute magnitude—but this sensitivity has not been rigorously quantified through systematic analysis.

### Computational Assumptions

Finite element mesh quality is maintained through structured generation algorithms that produce well-conditioned elements with aspect ratios near unity and interior angles avoiding extremes. Highly distorted elements after deformation could degrade solution accuracy, but the penetration constraint prevents deformations large enough to cause significant element distortion. The FEM solution is assumed converged to numerical precision, which is guaranteed for direct sparse solvers up to double precision arithmetic limits of approximately 10^-12 relative error. No iterative solver tolerance parameters require tuning, eliminating a potential source of solution variability.

## Conclusion

### Summary of Progress and Achievements

This comprehensive exam presents Palpation-Assisted Tomography, a computational framework for reconstructing tissue stiffness distributions from boundary displacement measurements using physics-informed neural networks. The work demonstrates a novel mesh-based PINN architecture that decouples forward and inverse problems, achieving computational efficiency through sparse linear algebra while maintaining physical rigor through exact equilibrium satisfaction. Computational cost reductions of 10 to 100 times compared to iterative optimization methods make the approach practical for systematic exploration of parameter space and eventual real-time applications.

### Central Innovations and Their Significance

Three methodological innovations distinguish PAT-Scan from prior inverse elastography work. The problem reformulation from continuous material field estimation to geometric segmentation for two-component systems reduces dimensionality dramatically while maintaining clinical relevance for tumor detection and defect localization. This reformulation recognizes that many diagnostic questions concern discrete boundaries rather than continuous fields—"Is there a tumor? Where? How big?"—and exploits this structure through binary segmentation leveraging mature medical image analysis techniques.

The mesh-based PINN architecture represents a distinct paradigm within physics-informed machine learning. Unlike meshfree PINNs that embed PDEs in the loss function through automatic differentiation, solving forward and inverse problems simultaneously at substantial computational cost, the mesh-based approach delegates the well-posed forward problem to established finite element solvers while reserving neural networks for the ill-posed inverse mapping. This decoupling achieves computational efficiency through sparse linear algebra, strengthens physical guarantees through exact equilibrium satisfaction, and enables modular development where advances in either component proceed independently.

The systematic interrogation strategy inspired by computed tomography applies forces sequentially at varied angular positions, progressively enriching the information content of boundary measurements. Universal geometry handling allows the same algorithm to function across circular, elliptical, and irregular inclusion shapes without modification, with level-set post-processing extracting boundaries from soft predictions for arbitrary topologies. This generality proves essential for handling realistic tumor geometries that defy simple parametric description.

These innovations combine to demonstrate that geometric inverse problems in elasticity can be solved efficiently using mesh-based physics-informed neural networks, that irregular geometries are handled without geometric assumptions through level-set methods, and that synthetic finite element-generated training data provides sufficient physics fidelity for proof-of-concept development. The algorithmic foundation is established; experimental validation represents the critical next step.

The framework extends naturally beyond tissue mechanics to other solid mechanics inverse problems where boundary measurements must reveal interior heterogeneity. ### Current Limitations and Path Forward

Current limitations include restriction to two-dimensional plane stress geometries, though three-dimensional extension follows established finite element procedures and presents engineering challenges rather than fundamental barriers. All validation currently occurs on synthetic data without exposure to real measurement noise, systematic errors from hardware imperfections, or biological variability in tissue properties. This represents the most critical limitation—the gap between synthetic proof-of-concept and experimental validation.

Stiffness underestimation by factors of 1.25 to 1.67 (recovered values 60-80% of ground truth) appears consistently across all reconstructions. This likely reflects the fundamental ill-posedness of boundary-only measurements that strongly constrain geometry but weakly constrain absolute stiffness magnitude. For clinical tumor detection, geometric localization and relative stiffness contrast may prove more diagnostically relevant than precise Young's modulus quantification, partially mitigating this limitation.

The requirement for boundary displacement completeness constrains experimental system design. Partial boundary coverage significantly degrades accuracy, demanding multi-angle measurement access through rotating stages, mirror systems, or synchronized camera arrays. This is not a fundamental limitation of the algorithm but rather a practical consideration for hardware implementation.

The path forward proceeds through experimental validation with silicone tissue-mimicking phantoms as specified in Aim 3c. Success in Phase 1 phantom experiments—achieving Dice coefficients exceeding 0.75 and boundary errors below 10% on physical specimens with realistic measurement noise—would validate that synthetic training transfers to real measurements. This would justify proceeding to Phase 2 ex-vivo tissue testing and potentially Phase 3 clinical feasibility studies, though the latter likely lies beyond PhD scope.
